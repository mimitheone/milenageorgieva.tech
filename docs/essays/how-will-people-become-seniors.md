# How will people become seniors if nobody gives them a junior role?

I keep reading about companies laying off employees and pointing to AI as the reason. Meanwhile, opportunities for interns and juniors seem increasingly limited.

Recently, I checked a job platform in Bulgaria. What I saw made me think about the people trying to enter the profession today. You need experience to get a job, but you need someone to give you a chance to gain that experience.

So the question keeps coming up:

Where will the next generation of senior professionals come from if we close the door to beginners?

It is a valid question. But I am increasingly frustrated with how often the discussion ends there.

As a professional who started an Academy from a blank sheet of paper, I have an opinion about what companies can do.

As someone who has consistently focused on employee development, I have an opinion about what managers should take responsibility for.

But as an individual, my response is more direct:

Please stop asking who will fix this and start working on the part you can fix.

Education and supporting new joiners are my personal mission. I dedicate my time, effort and skills to helping people grow. I set goals for that work, follow through and reach them regularly.

This is a responsibility I have chosen to take.

That is why I struggle with conversations where everyone can describe the problem, everyone can explain why it matters, and somehow nobody sees a role for themselves in solving it.

We need to discuss what is happening, challenge decisions and make their consequences visible. But recognising a problem cannot be the final contribution of everyone who understands it.

At some point, we need to start doing.

Companies cannot expect experience to appear on demand.

If your hiring strategy depends entirely on finding people whom another company has already trained, you depend on someone else continuing to make that investment.

What happens when everyone makes the same decision?

And please, stop treating "there are plenty of people on the market, but they do not have the right skills" as a complete explanation.

If that is where your analysis ends, you are missing how the job market works.

The skills available today reflect the opportunities people had to learn and work yesterday. Companies influence that supply through whom they hire, what they teach and which responsibilities they allow people to take on.

If we reduce entry-level hiring, cut development opportunities and then complain that candidates lack experience, we need to examine our own contribution to that outcome.

Of course, skills gaps exist. Some roles require expertise that cannot be developed during a short onboarding period. But we should be able to distinguish between essential knowledge, skills someone can learn with reasonable support and familiarity with our particular tools or internal processes.

Those distinctions matter. So do the salary offered, the location, the working conditions and the time we are willing to invest in someone.

An unfilled vacancy does not, by itself, prove that the people available are inadequate.

Before repeating that "the right people are not out there," ask how realistic your expectations are, what potential you are overlooking and what your organisation is prepared to develop.

Then there is the contradiction inside the organisation itself.

Someone is writing a job description asking for senior expertise, while someone else rejects a candidate for being "too senior."

One team says it cannot find enough experience. Another turns experience away.

There can be legitimate concerns about scope, salary or expectations. Then name them precisely and discuss them with the candidate. "Too senior" is a label that explains very little on its own.

When an organisation complains about a shortage of skills while rejecting the people who possess them, it needs to examine how its hiring decisions are being made.

At that point, I would stop treating this as an isolated symptom. It points to a serious disconnect between workforce planning, recruitment and management. It requires action.

Review the requirements. Examine why candidates are being rejected. Check whether the advertised responsibilities match the actual authority and compensation. Make sure the people defining the role and those assessing candidates agree on what they need.

Pointing at the job market is easy. Taking responsibility for contradictory decisions inside your own organisation requires work.

We want senior professionals with technical depth, sound judgement, business understanding and the ability to take responsibility. Those qualities develop through real work, feedback, mistakes, difficult decisions and gradually increasing responsibility.

Someone has to provide the conditions in which that development can happen.

If AI changes the work available to beginners, we need to reconsider how beginners learn within a company. Which fundamentals must they master? What should they be able to explain when using AI? How do we assess whether they understand a solution, can test it and can recognise when it is wrong?

These are practical questions we can work on.

Closing the entry point leaves us with the same development problem and fewer people gaining the experience to solve tomorrow's problems.

Managers have to be involved in people's development.

You cannot develop someone you never make an effort to understand.

If you never speak with a person beyond assigning work, never ask what they find difficult, never explore their ambitions and never discuss their reasoning, how do you know what they need to grow?

How do you distinguish a lack of knowledge from a lack of confidence? How do you recognise potential that has not yet had an opportunity to become visible?

A status update gives you information about a task. Understanding the person requires a conversation.

Ask questions. Listen to the answers. Give specific feedback. Explain your expectations. Provide a challenge that stretches someone's abilities, then make yourself available when they need guidance.

You do not become an exceptional manager simply because you can identify what everyone else is doing wrong.

Your contribution becomes visible in the team you build, the capabilities you develop and the people who become ready to take greater responsibility through working with you.

That takes involvement.

Seeing a light at the end of the tunnel is encouraging. Helping people move towards it requires work.

Development needs a place in the actual plan.

"People are our greatest asset" is an easy sentence to write.

Developing people requires time from experienced colleagues, meaningful assignments and acceptance that someone learning a task will initially need support.

If every available hour must produce immediate output, where exactly is development supposed to happen?

Starting an Academy from a blank sheet of paper made this very concrete for me. You have to decide what people need to learn, who will support them, how they will practise, how progress will be evaluated and what opportunity follows the training.

You have to connect learning to real work. You have to follow through when someone struggles. You have to adjust when your first approach does not work.

That experience shapes my expectations of leadership.

A manager may not control the company's entire recruitment strategy. But within their authority, they may be able to create a supervised assignment, establish a mentoring arrangement, involve a new joiner in a technical discussion or challenge a requirement that unnecessarily excludes beginners.

Where approval is needed, someone can build the case: the work involved, the support required, the cost and the expected outcome.

The constraints are real. So is the responsibility to examine what remains possible within them.

Ego does very little to help someone grow.

If every interaction becomes an opportunity to demonstrate how much more you know, a beginner quickly learns that asking questions carries a cost.

Then we complain that people do not ask enough questions.

Experienced professionals should be able to challenge someone's thinking without making the conversation a demonstration of superiority. We should be able to explain a mistake and help someone understand how to avoid it.

There is no loss of status in helping another person become capable.

For me, professional credibility comes from the quality of your work and the contribution you make to the people around you. It grows when you share knowledge, follow through on commitments and help others accomplish things they could not previously do.

Being the most experienced person in the room gives you an opportunity to be useful. What you do with that opportunity matters.

You do not need to build an entire Academy to contribute.

Not everyone can hire. Not everyone can commit to regular mentoring. But many of us can do something concrete.

Review a beginner's project properly. Help someone prepare for an interview. Explain why a design decision will create maintenance problems. Show a new joiner how you investigate a failure. Give feedback they can act on.

Move beyond "this is wrong" and explain the reasoning.

Ask why they chose an approach, how they tested it and which assumptions it depends on. Give them space to think, revise and try again.

These interactions help people develop judgement. They also communicate that learning is part of belonging to the team.

And that matters for new joiners at every experience level. Even an experienced professional needs context, access to people and the opportunity to ask questions when entering an unfamiliar environment.

Hiring someone is the beginning of building that relationship.

People entering the profession also have a part to play.

A difficult job market is not a personal failure. A motivated beginner cannot create a vacancy or approve a hiring budget.

But there are still things they can do while trying to get through the door.

Build something useful. Keep working on it after the first successful demonstration. Test it, ask someone to use it, investigate what breaks and explain your decisions.

Use AI, but make sure you understand and can defend the result.

Seek feedback and act on it. Ask questions that help you move forward. Take responsibility for the learning you can control.

None of this guarantees an interview or a job. It does create progress and evidence of your capabilities.

The same principle applies across experience levels: recognise the constraints, then identify your next useful action.

I want to hear what someone changed in their onboarding process. How they helped a struggling colleague improve. How they redesigned an internship. What they learned from mentoring someone whose needs were different from their own.

Share the attempts, including the ones that needed adjustment. Give other people something they can learn from and use.

I have chosen my contribution. Education and new joiners are where I invest my time, effort and skills. I have goals, I work towards them and I see results. There is always more to improve, but the work is happening.

I do not expect everyone to adopt my personal mission. I do expect us to recognise that progress requires participation.

If we want strong teams, we have to build them. If we want capable professionals, we have to contribute to their development.

So yes, ask where the next generation of seniors will come from.

Then ask yourself what you are doing to help someone become one.
